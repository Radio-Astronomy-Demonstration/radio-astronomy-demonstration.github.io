The science behind the RAD kit

The Origins of the Universe

Looking into the past

The history of Earth is incredibly rich and diverse. While humans appeared on this planet as early as 300,000 years ago, we arrived relatively late in our planet's development. If we go back even earlier, 100 million years ago dinosaurs roamed the Earth alongside our small mammalian ancestors. Going even further back, 4 and a half billion years ago, the Earth was in what we now call the Hadean Eon. During this time, the very young Earth faced continuous meteorite bombardment, and most of the surface was covered in lava.

If we want to look further back in history, before the formation of the Earth, we must turn our attention to outer space. A very important fact for astronomers is that light does not travel instantly, but rather moves through space at a fixed (but incredibly high) speed. In particular, we call the distance that light travels in a year a "light-year". If we point our telescopes at an object which is 1 light-year away, then by the time the light from that object reaches us, one year will have passed, and so we will see the object as it was one year ago. Therefore, by using powerful telescopes to look very far away from Earth, astronomers are able to see the universe's past.

The Big Bang

To the best of our knowledge, the universe began around 13.8 billion years ago in an event we call the Big Bang. At this time, the universe was an unimaginably hot, dense "soup" of particles. Importantly, this soup contained both negatively charged electrons and positively charged protons, the building blocks of elements such as hydrogen.

Much like the North and South poles of a magnet, oppositely-charged protons and electrons tend to attract one another. However, at the time of the Big Bang, these particles had very high energies, and moved too quickly to be able to actually bond with each other and form atoms. Therefore, right after it formed, the universe was full of hot, free-moving charged particles. This state of matter is known as plasma, and exists today inside of stars such as the Sun.

Over time, the universe expanded, allowing the particles to cool down and lose speed. Around 378,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe was finally cold enough to allow electrons to orbit protons. Thus, the very first atoms were formed, and the universe was no longer filled with plasma. We call this event recombination, since it is the first time that protons and electrons combined to form atoms.

The cosmic Dark Ages

After recombination, the universe was essentially full of newly formed cool gas. While this gas would eventually collapse in on itself, forming stars and planets, this is a very slow process, taking between 200 and 400 million years to form the first stars. If you could travel back to the universe right after recombination, you would see no stars, no planets, and no galaxies. In fact, there would be nothing in the entire universe which would produce any light that you could see at all. Because of this, astronomers call this period the Dark Ages of the universe.

Studying our Past

Seeing the invisible

When you or I want to get information about a faraway object, we typically use our eyes to see the light that the object emits or reflects. By merely seeing an object with our eyes we are able to make guesses about its shape, size, colour, distance, material, and so on, without needing to get close to it. Similarly, astronomers use telescopes to see the light from stars, galaxies, and other objects to determine their properties and behaviours. Therefore, you might expect that since no visible light is produced during the Dark Ages, we would have no way of exploring that time period. Luckily, however, this is not quite the case. As you may know, light is a wave, and our eyes can only see a small portion of the full spectrum of light. As it turns out, while the gas of the Dark Ages does not emit any visible light, it does emit a faint glow of radio light which we can still see today. By tuning telescopes to this specific type of light, astronomers are able to study how this gas behaved during the Dark Ages.

Technical difficulties

Unfortunately, there are a number of factors which make observing the radio light from the Dark Ages quite difficult. On average, a single atom of gas will only release a photon (a particle of light) every 10 million years, so the light we see is extremely faint. In fact, if our eyes could see radio light, then an entire balloon full of gas would seem just as bright as a lightbulb placed over 200km away! To make matters even worse, this extremely faint signal is then diluted and drowned out by a number of other factors. As you may have guessed from the name, radio light is also used by radio stations and other broadcasting systems here on Earth to wirelessly transmit music, sports coverage, news reports, and all sorts of other practical and entertainment media. Unfortunately, these signals are much stronger than the light from space we are trying to observe. Much like trying to have a conversation in a loud, crowded room, the noise from these Earth-based signals tend to drown out any quieter signals. Because of this, radio astronomers must build their detectors far away from any towns or cities. Our experiments, ALBATROS and MIST, both use sites in Uapishka, Québec, and Central Axel Heiberg Island, Nunavut. Both of these sites are far enough away from any interference that we can see the radio signal from outer space.

Unfortunately, even detectors built far away from radio noise sources cannot completely avoid interference. If you have ever noticed how a drinking straw looks warped, angled, or "broken" when placed in water, then you have observed refraction. This is caused by the fact that light slows down when it enters water, which causes it change direction. As it turns out, there is a layer of Earth's upper atmosphere, called the ionosphere, which tends to refract radio light. Furthermore, much like a prism, the amount of refraction depends on the specific frequency of light.
