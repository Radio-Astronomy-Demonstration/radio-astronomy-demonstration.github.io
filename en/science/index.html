The science behind the RAD kit

The Origins of the Universe

Looking into the past

The history of Earth is incredibly rich and diverse. While humans appeared on this planet as early as 300,000 years ago, we arrived relatively late in our planet's development. If we go back even earlier, 100 million years ago dinosaurs roamed the Earth alongside our small mammalian ancestors. Going even further back, 4 and a half billion years ago, the Earth was in what we now call the Hadean Eon. During this time, the very young Earth faced continuous meteorite bombardment, and most of the surface was covered in lava.

If we want to look further back in history, before the formation of the Earth, we must turn our attention to outer space. A very important fact for astronomers is that light does not travel instantly, but rather moves through space at a fixed (but incredibly high) speed. In particular, we call the distance that light travels in a year a "light-year". If we point our telescopes at an object which is 1 light-year away, then by the time the light from that object reaches us, one year will have passed, and so we will see the object as it was one year ago. Therefore, by using powerful telescopes to look very far away from Earth, astronomers are able to see the universe's past.

The Big Bang

To the best of our knowledge, the universe began around 13.8 billion years ago in an event we call the Big Bang. At this time, the universe was an unimaginably hot, dense "soup" of particles. Importantly, this soup contained both negatively charged electrons and positively charged protons, the building blocks of elements such as hydrogen.

Much like the North and South poles of a magnet, oppositely-charged protons and electrons tend to attract one another. However, at the time of the Big Bang, these particles had very high energies, and moved too quickly to be able to actually bond with each other and form atoms. Therefore, right after it formed, the universe was full of hot, free-moving charged particles. This state of matter is known as plasma, and exists today inside of stars such as the Sun.

Over time, the universe expanded, allowing the particles to cool down and lose speed. Around 375,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe was finally cold enough to allow electrons to orbit protons, forming the first ever hydrogen atoms.
